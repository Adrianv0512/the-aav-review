Consider a conversation in which two people agree that a policy should be “fair.” It sounds like common ground. But one might mean treating everyone identically, while the other means accounting for different starting points.

The word is doing two different jobs. Until that becomes clear, the conversation can circle without getting closer to the disagreement underneath it.

This is an illustrative example, not a claim about a particular policy or group. It suggests a habit that can make a piece of opinion writing easier to follow: begin by asking what the key words mean.

## Three questions to keep nearby

1. **What is being claimed?** Try stating the idea in one sentence without adding an argument against it.
2. **What would count as evidence?** Identify what could support the claim, weaken it, or leave it unresolved.
3. **Where does a value judgment enter?** People can agree about a consequence and disagree about how much it matters.

These questions do not produce an automatic answer. They make the shape of a disagreement easier to see.

## A source is a starting point

Linking a source is useful, but a link does not explain itself. A reader still needs to know which part supports the sentence, what the source measured, and what remains an interpretation.

For example, an author researching US federal legislation can begin with [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/), then link to the specific bill being discussed. The exact source matters more than the prestige of a general homepage.

A compact distinction helps:

- **Fact:** a claim that can be checked against evidence.
- **Analysis:** an explanation of what that evidence may mean.
- **Opinion:** a judgment about what is desirable, important, or worth doing.

In practice, a paragraph may contain all three. Good writing does not need to label every sentence, but it should make the transitions understandable.

## The possibility of revision

One useful question to ask of an opinion is: what might change it?

The answer could be new evidence. It could also be a clearer understanding of a tradeoff or of another person’s experience. Sometimes the honest answer is that a view rests on a value, and evidence alone will not settle it.

That does not make the conversation pointless. It makes the terms of the conversation clearer.

> Being able to explain a disagreement is a worthwhile result, even when nobody changes sides.

A notebook is a useful format for this kind of work. It can hold an argument today and a correction tomorrow. It can leave a question open without disguising that openness as a conclusion.

There will be time for an opinion. First, it is worth making sure we are answering the right question.
